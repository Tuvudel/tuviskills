# Principles and language

Read this reference for substantial prose, rewrites, and editorial reviews.

## Voice and tone

- Write in a conversational, friendly, respectful voice without slang, frivolity, or forced entertainment.
- Let personality show, but keep useful information primary.
- Avoid excessive politeness in instructions. Don't add _please_ to every step.
- Use contractions naturally in English.
- Avoid exclamation marks except when the content genuinely requires one.

## Sentence construction

- Prefer subject-verb-object order.
- Use active voice when identifying the actor helps the reader. Passive voice is acceptable when the actor is irrelevant, unknown, or intentionally de-emphasized.
- Use present tense for current behavior. Don't use future tense for an outcome that follows immediately from an action.
- Put conditional clauses before instructions.
- Keep modifiers next to the terms they modify. Avoid long noun stacks.
- Avoid double negatives and exceptions to exceptions.
- Vary sentence openings enough to avoid a mechanical series of _You can_ or _To..._ statements.

## Literal wording and explicit meaning

Use these examples as local writing guidance, inspired by ASD-STE100 clarity principles. They do not demonstrate formal compliance.

- **Decorative metaphor:** Replace _This setting is a dial worth turning_ with _Try changing this setting_. If known, explain which behavior the setting controls.
- **Mannered evaluation:** Replace _This point earns its keep_ with _This point still matters_, then state why when the context supports it.
- **Unclear action:** Replace _A service restart is required after a configuration change_ with _If you change the configuration, restart the service_.
- **Hidden actor:** Replace _Your request will be processed_ with _The server processes your request_ only if the server is the known actor. Preserve uncertainty when the source does not identify the actor.
- **Connected explanation:** Prefer _The service reads its settings at startup. If you change the configuration, restart the service to apply the new settings_ when that behavior is established. Do not invent the startup behavior merely to improve the explanation.

Retain an analogy when it explains an unfamiliar concept more accurately or efficiently than literal wording alone. State the actual mechanism and relevant limits. Preserve an explicitly requested creative voice.

## Reader and user terminology

- Use _you_ for the reader.
- Use _user_ for a person who uses the product that the reader develops, unless the reader and product user are the same person.
- Use first person for the writer or organization only when it identifies an action, recommendation, or position.

## Global and inclusive language

- Prefer short, literal words with one likely meaning.
- Avoid idioms, humor that depends on culture, seasonal references, and current popular culture.
- Avoid phrasal verbs when a simpler single verb is natural, but retain established terms such as _set up_ and _log in_.
- Don't use disability, violence, mental health, gender, race, age, or socioeconomic status as a casual metaphor.
- Use diverse, realistic examples without stereotypes.
- Preserve articles such as _a_, _an_, and _the_; don't drop them to make headings artificially short.
- Avoid Latin abbreviations such as _e.g._, _i.e._, and _etc._ in ordinary prose. Use _for example_, _that is_, or wording that makes a list nonexhaustive.

## Abbreviations and terminology

- Spell out an abbreviation on first use when the audience might not know it, followed immediately by the abbreviation in parentheses.
- If an abbreviation appears only once and isn't as familiar as the full term, omit the abbreviation.
- If the first use is in a heading, define it in the first following paragraph.
- Don't use abbreviations, product names, or UI labels as verbs.
- Base _a_ versus _an_ on pronunciation, not spelling.
- Check the live word list for exact spelling, capitalization, compound words, and terms marked _Don't use_ or _Use with caution_.

## Recommendations and modality

- Required action: use _must_, _need_, or an imperative.
- Optional action or permission: use _can_.
- Possible outcome: use _might_ or _can_.
- Expected outcome: state the outcome directly.
- Recommendation: use _we recommend_ or identify the recommending authority.
- Policy or legal permission: use _may_ only when that formal meaning is intended.
- Avoid _should_ when it could mean required, recommended, expected, or merely probable.
